Biography

Juan Pablo Montalva Peroni is a Chilean director, photographer and visual artist, born in the coastal city of Viña del Mar on the shores of the Pacific Ocean. From an early age, he has maintained a profound connection to his natural and social surroundings, a relationship that has significantly shaped his aesthetic vision and development as a visual storyteller. Influenced by the sound of the sea, the wind, and the subaltern narratives inherent to human existence, Montalva has developed an artistic voice that explores the tensions between the everyday and the sacred, between fragility and hope.

Over the past decade, his work has been exhibited in major cultural institutions, including the Museum of Contemporary Art in Santiago, and has been presented in both solo and group exhibitions across Chile, Argentina, the United States, Germany, Italy, England and Japan. Among his most notable projects is a portrait series produced in 2015 documenting the punk subculture of Valparaíso, which was later presented in solo exhibitions at Galería La Sala (2018) and Galería Animal (2023). This body of work, like much of his practice, is characterised by a documentary approach that combines aesthetic exploration, social research and an ethical commitment to the communities and individuals portrayed.

His artistic production includes the documentary short film The Squat (2020), which explores the experiences of Colombian migrants living in an informal settlement built within the remains of the former Yungay railway station in Santiago. Within the fields of cinema and moving-image work, Montalva directed the fashion film The Dream (2024) for L’Officiel, starring acclaimed Chilean actress Patricia Rivadeneira, a project that was officially selected for the Milano Fashion Film Festival. Other significant works include The Cosmos’ Heart (2020), a dance film produced during the pandemic, featuring music by German artist ATOM™ (Uwe Schmidt) and performed by dancer Alexandra Mabes, as well as his most recent venture into fiction, Stolen Summer (2025), a coming-of-age story set in 1990s Viña del Mar. Based on the novel Verano Robado by María José Viera-Gallo and starring Paulina Moreno and Roque Artiagoitia, the film received the award for Best Directorial Debut at the Manchester festival in England.

In the commercial sphere, Montalva has built a distinguished career as both director and photographer, collaborating with international brands including Levi’s, H&M, Nike, Volkswagen, Peugeot, Zero + Maria Cornejo, L’Oréal, Netflix and Polo Ralph Lauren. He has also photographed internationally recognised figures such as Sean Lennon, Gael García Bernal, Helen Fisher, Yoko Ono, Pedro Lemebel, Daniela Vega, Joan Collins, Bobby Gillespie (Primal Scream), Colombina Parra and acclaimed Argentine actor Ricardo Darín. His work in advertising has been recognised with awards at the London International Advertising Festival and the Mallorca Festival in Spain.

After living for six years in New York and five years in Manchester, England, Montalva currently divides his time between Chile and Mexico, where he lives and works. From this position, he continues to develop projects that move fluidly between documentary narrative, fiction and contemporary visual creation, always guided by an approach that combines social enquiry, aesthetic exploration and reflection on the human experience.

THE CHAPTERS

The Chapters is a narrative study of shifting interior states. Dance, desire, love, doubt, loss and despair unfold as fragments of a single human experience, embodied by acclaimed Chilean actors Mariana Di Girolamo, Marcelo Alonso, Alfredo Castro and José Soza. Separate gestures, bodies and moments are drawn into a loose chronology — a story told imperfectly, perhaps necessarily so, because memory itself has no obligation to sequence. We remember in fragments: an image precedes its cause, a gesture outlives the event that produced it, a body, a room or a particular quality of light may return years later, detached from its original context yet charged with its emotional residue. The images we see do not belong to the present either. By the time something becomes visible, it has already begun to recede. Light requires time to reach us; even what we call ‘now’ contains an infinitesimal distance from what has just taken place. Astronomers look into the night sky and see the past: light that has travelled across immense distances before reaching the eye. They look backwards in order to understand the present. Patricio Guzmán’s Nostalgia for the Light inhabits a similar territory, where the act of looking becomes a means of searching through absence, memory and what remains. In The Chapters, however, that distance is not measured in light-years, but within the intimate scale of human experience: a movement, a touch, a hesitation, a fall, a moment of tenderness, an absence. Everything we feel begins to disappear at the very moment it occurs. The camera comes afterwards. It gathers traces, rearranges them and gives them another duration. The Chapters does not seek to construct a closed narrative, but to inhabit the unstable space between experience and memory, presence and disappearance. Like memory, some images remain precise while others erode; some vanish, while others return with inexplicable force. Perhaps this is also how we inhabit the present: perpetually attempting to understand something that is already becoming the past. The Chapters is, ultimately, a meditation on human feeling and on the fragile interval between living an experience and recognising that it has already begun to disappear.

The Yeta System Project

The project was born in Valparaíso, in Chile’s Fifth Region, and explores the visual culture of punk and its ramifications across urban, coastal and forested margins. The term Yeta, meaning “bad luck”, becomes a metaphor for the marginalised, the rejected, but also the resilient.
The Yeta System is a constellation of codes, gestures and aesthetics constructed by young punks in response to an adverse environment, documented through photography both as an archive and as an autonomous visual language.
The project records not only faces and bodies, but also contexts: the sea, the concrete, the precariousness of inhabited spaces, street symbols and the spontaneous performances of the punks themselves. The Yeta System becomes a visual chronicle of Valparaíso’s counterculture, imbued with a sense of ritual, resistance and community.
A chronicle of those who choose to exist beyond the boundaries of the system, declaring: here I am, on my own terms. A refusal to conform, a refusal to become what others expect.
These images reveal a ferocious Latin American species, one that takes English symbols and reclaims them — each garment transformed into a philosophical manifesto, each gesture a way of seeing the world.
It could belong to a Shakespearean scene: a stage beyond time. Punk suspended between eras, unbound by social order and untethered to any particular historical moment.
A species that resists classification.
A species that resists extinction.

—Colombina Parra

The Feast of Saint Peter

Patron Saint of Fishermen

The Feast of Saint Peter, patron saint of fishermen, erupts every 29 June like a salt-soaked miracle in the ports and fishing coves of Chile’s Fifth Region. But it is in Valparaíso that the whole affair becomes living flesh, sea foam and drumbeat.
There, the saint emerges from the water — or rather, they bring him out, stiff in his sacred image, perched upon a boat sailing amongst mended nets, plastic garlands and flags that the wind lovingly frays.
The entire harbour becomes a procession: the boats sound their sirens like sea beasts calling upon their working-class god. The fishing boats, poor and proud, dress themselves in glossy paper, recycled bottles and artificial flowers that somehow still smell of faith. For that is what hangs in the air: a briny faith, a hope clinging to the ebb and flow of the sea.
Weather-beaten men beg the saint not to fail them, beg the ocean not to swallow them whole, beg the fish to bite again. They pray with hearts full of salt and debt, with hands split open from hauling in the nets. And amongst those prayers, gratitude slips in too — that secret tenderness the sea sometimes bestows.
When the maritime procession ends, Saint Peter climbs the hill, like a weary old man returning home. They carry him on their shoulders through the damp streets of the port, amongst Virgins, drums and women weeping with shining eyes.
At the Church of La Artillería, the Chinos await him — those men dressed in centuries, dancing with little bells and whips, with a devotion that smells of sweat and promises.
The whole harbour kneels a little that day.
Not before the saint, but before the sea.
That unpredictable god who feeds them, punishes them, rocks them, erases them.
And amongst sirens and prayers, Valparaíso beats like a wet heart, covered in nets and faith.

—Pedro Lemebel
